## Chapter Summary

Chapter 6 develops first-order (paraxial) optics systematically from the paraxial ray equations of Chapter 5 into a complete design toolkit. It introduces the **systematic ray trace format** (§6.2) that reduces any multi-surface system to two pre-computed constants per surface; derives the **optical invariant** (§6.3), which is conserved through every surface and space; proves the **linearity theorem** (§6.4), showing that any paraxial ray is a linear combination of any two independent reference rays; derives all six **cardinal points** (§6.5) — focal points, principal planes, and nodal points — and both the Gaussian and Newtonian image equations; extends the formalism to **thin lens systems** (§6.7) and **mirror systems** (§6.8); presents the **differential coefficient** method (§6.9) for computing sensitivity of focal length to parameter changes; and treats **chromatic aberration** (§6.10) including axial (TAch) and lateral (Tch) color, the thin-lens achromatic doublet conditions, secondary spectrum, and the Petzval connection. Chapter 6 concludes with entrance/exit pupils, the chief ray, and vignetting (§6.11).

**Key engineering takeaways:**
- The paraxial ray trace format (Table 6.1) reduces any system to alternating c(n₋₁−n) and t/n constants — two rows per surface/space — making the trace mechanical and self-checking.
- Two independent paraxial rays (axial + chief) completely characterize all first-order properties; any third ray is a linear combination (§6.4, Gem 6.2).
- Graphical paraxial tracing is accurate to ~1%; use numerical tracing for any quantitative result (Gem 6.3).
- The achromatic doublet condition: $\phi_a/\nu_a + \phi_b/\nu_b = 0$, giving $\phi_a = \phi\nu_a/(\nu_a-\nu_b)$, $\phi_b = -\phi\nu_b/(\nu_a-\nu_b)$ — a direct formula for glass power split.
- Secondary spectrum (residual chromatic after F-C achromatization) scales as $f/2200$ for typical glass pairs and is nearly independent of glass choice.
- For mirrors: use $n_{j+1} = -n_j$ to fold the refraction equations — the entire mirror system can be traced as a sequence of refracting surfaces.

## 6.1 General

**6.1.1 First order optics and paraxial rays.** In §5.11.2 it was pointed out that when the sine of the angle is replaced by the angle, the resulting equations belong to the field of first order optics. In general, if any trigonometric function is replaced by its first approximation, we get first order equations in the field of optics. In §§5.9.1 and 5.9.2 a paraxial ray was defined as one differentially displaced from the optical axis. Because of this definition the first approximation to trigonometric functions must be used, and the resulting paraxial ray equations are identical to the first order equations.

**6.1.2 Preliminary layout and graphical ray trace.** The method of tracing paraxial rays graphically was explained in §5.10. Graphical ray tracing is extremely useful in the preliminary design stage, particularly for complicated systems which cannot be visualized easily. The designer can thereby get a "feel" for the system that a mere array of numbers often hides. Graphical ray tracing is, however, limited to an accuracy of about **one percent**. For additional accuracy — which is absolutely necessary in the calculation of aberrations — numerical paraxial ray tracing must be used. The methods and results of this type of ray tracing in the realm of first order optics will be discussed in §6.

## 6.2 Numerical Tracing of Paraxial Rays

**6.2.1 Importance of paraxial ray tracing.** The accurate numerical tracing of paraxial rays is used extensively in the design of optical systems for three main reasons:

1. Tracing paraxial rays through the system is a simple mathematical procedure.
2. Images formed by paraxial rays provide very convenient reference planes.
3. Data obtained in paraxial ray calculations can be used to calculate the first approximation to image aberration.

For these reasons, a systematic method of numerical ray tracing of paraxial rays is a necessary tool for the designer, even today when large automatic computers are readily available.

## 6.3 The Optical Invariant

**6.3.1 Axial and oblique rays.** Two types of paraxial rays characterize any system:
- **Axial (marginal) paraxial ray:** starts from the object surface at $y_0 = 0$; crosses the axis at image surfaces. Traced with height $y$ and reduced angle $nu$.
- **Oblique (chief) paraxial ray:** starts from the object at $\bar{y}_0 \neq 0$; passes through the center of the aperture stop. Traced with height $\bar{y}$ and reduced angle $n\bar{u}$.

For both rays, the refraction and transfer equations are:

$$nu = n_{-1}u_{-1} + yc(n_{-1}-n) \tag{5-57a}$$

$$n\bar{u} = n_{-1}\bar{u}_{-1} + \bar{y}c(n_{-1}-n) \tag{5-57b}$$

$$y = y_{-1} + \frac{t_{-1}}{n_{-1}}(n_{-1}u_{-1}) \tag{5-56a}$$

$$\bar{y} = \bar{y}_{-1} + \frac{t_{-1}}{n_{-1}}(n_{-1}\bar{u}_{-1}) \tag{5-56b}$$

<!-- === PAGE 6 === -->

**6.3.2 The optical invariant and its importance.** From the combination of both ray equations, the following expression is constant at every surface and every space throughout the system:

$$\bar{y}(nu) - y(n\bar{u}) = \Phi \tag{6}$$

$\Phi$ is called the **optical invariant** (also Lagrange invariant or Smith-Helmholtz invariant). It may also be written in determinant form:

$$\Phi = \begin{vmatrix} \bar{y} & n\bar{u} \\ y & nu \end{vmatrix}$$

**6.3.3 The invariant for refraction.** By transposing and dividing Equations 5-(57a) and 5-(57b) to equate the common term $c(n_{-1}-n)$, then rearranging:

$$\bar{y}(nu) - y(n\bar{u}) = \bar{y}(n_{-1}u_{-1}) - y(n_{-1}\bar{u}_{-1}) \tag{5}$$

This shows $\Phi$ is the same on both sides of any surface.

**6.3.4 The invariant for transfer.** Similarly, combining Equations 5-(56a) and 5-(56b) shows $\Phi$ is the same in any space between surfaces.

**6.3.5 The invariant for the entire system.** Hence $\Phi$ is the same everywhere; in particular:

$$\Phi = \bar{y}_0 (n_0 u_0) - y_0 (n_0 \bar{u}_0) = \bar{y}_k (n_{k-1} u_{k-1}) - y_k (n_{k-1} \bar{u}_{k-1})$$

**6.3.6 Lateral magnification.** If $y_0 = 0$ (object on axis) and $y_k = 0$ (image on axis), then:

$$\Phi = \bar{y}_0 (n_0 u_0) = \bar{y}_k (n_{k-1} u_{k-1})$$

The **lateral magnification** $m$ is defined as:

$$m = \frac{\bar{y}_k}{\bar{y}_0} = \frac{n_0 u_0}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \tag{7}$$

**6.3.7 Angular magnification.**

**6.3.7.1** For systems with the object at infinity ($t_0 = \infty$, $u_0 = 0$): the ray coordinates at the first surface are $y_1$, $u_0 = 0$; $\bar{y}_1$, $\bar{u}_0$. The optical invariant becomes:

$$\Phi = -y_1 (n_0 \bar{u}_0)$$

In the image plane ($y_k = 0$):

$$\bar{y}_k = \frac{-y_1}{(n_{k-1} u_{k-1})} (n_0 \bar{u}_0) \tag{8}$$

For a **telescopic (afocal) system** (both object and image at infinity, $u_{k-1} = 0$):

$$\Phi = -y_1 (n_0 \bar{u}_0) = -y_k (n_{k-1} \bar{u}_{k-1})$$

**6.3.7.2** The **angular magnification** (magnifying power) of a telescope in afocal adjustment is:

$$\alpha = \frac{\bar{u}_{k-1}}{\bar{u}_0} = \frac{y_1 n_0}{y_k n_{k-1}} = MP \tag{9}$$

>[FIGURE: page 7, ~500×380px — Figure 6.3: Three diagrams illustrating Smith-Helmholtz equations. (a) Lateral image: object arrow ȳ₀ at left, image arrow ȳₖ at right, magnification m = ȳₖ/ȳ₀. **6.3.8 The Smith-Helmholtz and Lagrange equations.** Equations (7) and (8) can be rewritten as:

$$\bar{y}_0 n_0 u_0 = \bar{y}_k n_{k-1} u_{k-1}$$

and

$$y_1 n_0 \bar{u}_0 = -\bar{y}_k n_{k-1} u_{k-1}$$

These are referred to as the **Smith-Helmholtz equations** (or Lagrange equations by other writers). Through these equations it is possible to decide rapidly what is needed to set up a given optical system.

## 6.4 Linearity of the Paraxial Ray Trace Equations

**6.4.1 General.** In §§5.9.3 and 5.10 it was seen that finite heights and angles can be used with the paraxial ray trace equations, because these equations are linear. A consequence of this linearity is that if two rays are traced through an optical system, it is possible to predict the path of any other paraxial ray.

<!-- === PAGE 8 === -->

**6.4.2 Proof of the theorem.** Let $y$ and $\bar{y}$ be the heights of any two paraxial rays on the $j$th surface, with $u$ and $\bar{u}$ the corresponding angles. Any third ray $\tilde{y}$, $\tilde{u}$ satisfies:

$$A\bar{y} + By = \tilde{y} \tag{10a}$$

$$A\bar{u} + Bu = \tilde{u} \tag{10b}$$

for the entire optical system, where $A$ and $B$ are constants determined from the initial conditions. Solving these equations for $A$ and $B$:

$$A = \frac{\tilde{y}u - \tilde{u}y}{\bar{y}u - \bar{u}y} = \frac{n(\tilde{y}u - \tilde{u}y)}{\Phi}$$

$$B = \frac{\bar{y}\tilde{u} - \tilde{y}\bar{u}}{\bar{y}u - y\bar{u}} = \frac{n(\bar{y}\tilde{u} - \tilde{y}\bar{u})}{\Phi}$$

**6.4.3 Two particular rays.** Choosing the two reference rays as:
1. The **axial ray** from $y_0 = 0$ (intersects axis at object plane),
2. The **chief ray** from $\bar{y}_0 \neq 0$, intersecting the axis at the first surface ($\bar{y}_1 = 0$):

$$A = \tilde{y}_0 / \bar{y}_0, \quad B = \tilde{y}_1 / y_1 \tag{11}$$

$$B = \frac{-\tilde{y}_1 \bar{u}_0}{\bar{y}_0 u_0} \tag{12}$$

>[FIGURE: page 9, ~500×340px — Figure 6.4: Rays used to find simple expressions for A and B. Shows two surfaces (Surface 0 and Surface 1) separated by t₀. Three rays are shown: the axial ray (starting at y₀=0, ȳ₀≠0, going through with angle u₀), the chief ray (from ȳ₀≠0, passing through ȳ₁=0), and a general third ray (dotted). The combinations A and B for the linear combination are labeled geometrically.]

**6.4.3.2** In practice, the two particular rays are:
- **Rim (marginal) ray** — from axial object, through edge of aperture stop; determines lens diameters.
- **Chief ray** — from maximum field height, through center of aperture stop; determines field of view.

## 6.5 The Cardinal Points of an Optical System

**6.5.1 General.** Six special points on the optical axis — the **cardinal points** — completely characterize the first-order imaging of any centered optical system:

(a) First and second focal points: $F_1$ and $F_2$
(b) First and second principal points: $P_1$ and $P_2$
(c) First and second nodal points: $N_1$ and $N_2$

<!-- === PAGE 10 === -->

**6.5.2 The second focal point and second focal length.** Tracing an axial ray from $u_0 = 0$ (parallel to axis) through the system, it converges to $F_2$ — the **second focal point**. The **second focal length** is:

$$f' = -\frac{y_1}{u_{k-1}} \tag{13}$$

Physically, $f'$ is the distance from the second principal point $P_2$ to $F_2$.

>[FIGURE: page 11, ~490×300px — Figure 6.5: Location of second focal point F₂, second principal plane, and second focal length f'. Shows a multi-element system. A ray parallel to the axis (u₀=0) enters at height y₁, traverses the system, and exits at angle u_{k-1}, crossing the axis at F₂. The dashed extension of the exit ray back to the plane of the entering ray defines the second principal plane H₂ at P₂. Distance P₂F₂ = f'. Medium n₀ at left, n_{k-1} at right.]

**6.5.3 The second principal point.** $P_2$ is located at the intersection of the forward-extended entering ray and the backward-extended exit ray. The plane through $P_2$ perpendicular to the axis is the **second principal plane**, and $f' = P_2 F_2$.

**6.5.4 The second nodal point.** The second nodal point $N_2$ satisfies:

$$N_2 F_2 = (P_2 F_2) \frac{n_0}{n_{k-1}}$$

Hence:

$$\bar{y}_k = f' \frac{n_0 \bar{u}_0}{n_{k-1}} = P_2 F_2 \frac{n_0 \bar{u}_0}{n_{k-1}} = N_2 F_2 \cdot \bar{u}_0$$

**6.5.5 The first focal, principal, and nodal points.**

**6.5.5.1** The **first focal point** $F_1$ is such that rays entering from $F_1$ emerge parallel to the axis. The **first focal length** is:

$$f = \frac{y_k}{u_0} = F_1 P_1 \tag{14}$$

The first nodal point: $F_1 N_1 = (F_1 P_1) \frac{n_{k-1}}{n_0}$.

>[FIGURE: page 12, ~490×300px — Figure 6.6: Location of first focal point F₁, first principal plane, and second focal length f. <!-- === PAGE 12 === -->

**6.5.5.2** The **first focal plane** is the plane perpendicular to the axis at $F_1$. All rays parallel to the axis after emerging from the system, when traced backwards, pass through $F_1$.

**6.5.6 Object and image positions with respect to focal and principal points.**

**6.5.6.3** Four distances are used:
- $f$ — measured from $F_1$ to $P_1$
- $f'$ — measured from $P_2$ to $F_2$
- $Z$ — measured from object plane to $F_1$
- $Z'$ — measured from $F_2$ to image plane
- $S$ — measured from object plane to $P_1$
- $S'$ — measured from $P_2$ to image plane

All distances are positive to the right.

<!-- === PAGE 14 === -->

**6.5.6.4 Newtonian form (distances from focal points):**

$$m = -\frac{Z'}{f'} = -\frac{f}{Z} \tag{15}$$

$$ZZ' = ff' \tag{16}$$

**Gaussian form (distances from principal planes):**

$$\frac{f}{S} + \frac{f'}{S'} = 1 \tag{17}$$

$$m = -\frac{fS'}{f'S} \tag{18}$$

With $Z = S - f$ and $Z' = S' - f'$, these two forms are equivalent. When $f = f'$ (object and image in same medium), Eq. (17) becomes $1/S + 1/S' = 1/f'$, the standard thin-lens formula.

**6.5.7 Additional characteristics of principal planes.** Principal planes are planes of **unit positive magnification**: any ray intersecting $P_1$ at height $H_1$ exits the system at the same height from $P_2$. Hence any entering ray can be considered as deviated only once, at the principal plane.

**6.5.8 Additional characteristics of nodal points.**

**6.5.8.1** An important relation between focal lengths and refractive indices:

$$\frac{f}{n_0} = \frac{f'}{n_{k-1}} \tag{19}$$

**6.5.8.2** Cardinal point relations following from Eq. (19):

$$P_1 N_1 = P_2 N_2, \quad P_1 P_2 = N_1 N_2, \quad F_1 N_1 = f', \quad N_2 F_2 = f$$

When $n_0 = n_{k-1}$: $f = f'$, and the nodal points coincide with the principal points ($P_1 = N_1$, $P_2 = N_2$).

## 6.6 Calculation of Focal Length from Finite Conjugate Data

**6.6.1 General.** If either conjugate is at infinity, the entering axial ray is traced at $u_0 = 0$, $y_1$ arbitrary, and the focal length is given directly by Eq. (13).

**6.6.2 Finite conjugates.** If both conjugates are finite, an axial ray and an oblique paraxial ray are traced as shown in Figure 6.4 and Table 6.6. With $A = \bar{y}_0/\bar{y}_0$ and $B = -\bar{y}_1\bar{u}_0/(\bar{y}_0 u_0)$, the focal length is:

$$f' = -\frac{\Phi}{n_0 (u_0 \bar{u}_{k-1} - \bar{u}_0 u_{k-1})} \tag{20}$$

where $\Phi = \bar{y}_0 (n_0 u_0)$ from §6.3.6.

## 6.7 Systems of Thin Lenses in Air

**6.7.1 Concept of the thin lens.** When $n_0 = n_2 = 1$ (lens in air) and $t_1 \approx 0$ (negligible thickness), both principal planes and both nodal points coincide at the lens center. Equations (17) and (18) simplify to:

$$\frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{S'} = \frac{1}{f'}$$

$$m = -\frac{S'}{S}$$

**6.7.2 Focal length and power of a thin lens in air.** Tracing an axial paraxial ray through a thin two-surface element:

$$u_2 = u_0 - y(n-1)(c_1 - c_2) \tag{21}$$

The **lensmaker's equation** (power of thin lens):

$$\frac{1}{f'} = (n-1)(c_1 - c_2) = \phi \tag{22}$$

where $\phi$ is the **power** (diopters if $f'$ in meters; mm$^{-1}$ if $f'$ in mm).

## 6.9 Differential Changes in First Order Optics

**6.9.1 General.** In the second step of the design procedure — calculation of focal lengths and separations of individual elements — it is important to know how changes in curvature, thickness, and index affect the first-order solution. Formulae for computing these effects are given in §6.9.

**6.9.2 Determination of the differential coefficients.** A change of any parameter results in changes $dy_k$ and $du_{k-1}$ in the final ray trace output. These satisfy:

$$dy_k = \left(\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial y_{j+1}}\right) dy_{j+1} + \left(\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial u}\right) du \tag{26}$$

$$du_{k-1} = \left(\frac{\partial u_{k-1}}{\partial y_{j+1}}\right) dy_{j+1} + \left(\frac{\partial u_{k-1}}{\partial u}\right) du \tag{27}$$

<!-- === PAGE 23 === -->

The **differential coefficients** (partial derivatives) are obtained by tracing the axial ray $y$, $u$ and the oblique ray $\bar{y}$, $\bar{u}$ as two differential rays near the reference ray:

$$\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial y_{j+1}} = \frac{n(\bar{y}_k u - y_k \bar{u})}{\Phi} \tag{28}$$

$$\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial u} = \frac{n(y_k \bar{y}_{j+1} - \bar{y}_k y_{j+1})}{\Phi} \tag{29}$$

$$\frac{\partial u_{k-1}}{\partial y_{j+1}} = \frac{n(\bar{u}_{k-1} u - u_{k-1} \bar{u})}{\Phi} \tag{30}$$

$$\frac{\partial u_{k-1}}{\partial u} = \frac{n(\bar{y}_{j+1} u_{k-1} - y_{j+1} \bar{u}_{k-1})}{\Phi} \tag{31}$$

**6.9.3 Effect of curvature change on focal length.** For a curvature change $dc$ (with $t$ and $n$ held constant), using $dy_{j+1} = t\,du$:

$$dy_k = \frac{n}{\Phi} \left(y_k \bar{y} - \bar{y}_k y\right) du \tag{32}$$

$$du_{k-1} = \frac{n}{\Phi} \left(\bar{y} u_{k-1} - y \bar{u}_{k-1}\right) du \tag{33}$$

The differential of the focal length with respect to $c_j$:

$$\frac{df'}{dc} = \frac{\left[-f'(\bar{y} u_{k-1} - y \bar{u}_{k-1})\right]\left[y(n_{-1} - n)\right]}{\Phi u_{k-1}}$$

<!-- === PAGE 24 === -->

**6.9.4 Effect of curvature change on final angle.** Example: A curvature change $\Delta c_4 = 0.01$ on surface 4 of the Table 6.6 system. From Eq. (32) and Table 6.6 data:

$$\frac{du_6}{dc_4} = \frac{y_4(n_3 - n_4)}{\Phi}(\bar{y}_4 u_6 - y_4 \bar{u}_6) = 0.469$$

Therefore:

$$\Delta u_6 = \frac{du_6}{dc_4} \cdot \Delta c_4 = (0.469)(0.01) = 0.00469$$

**6.9.5 Effect of thickness change on final angle.** For a thickness change $dt$ (with $c$ and $n$ constant), $dy_{j+1} = u\,dt$, and from Eqs. (27) and (30):

$$\frac{du_{k-1}}{dt} = \frac{nu}{\Phi}\left[\bar{u}_{k-1} u - u_{k-1} \bar{u}\right]$$

## 6.10 Chromatic Aberration

**6.10.1 The meaning of chromatic aberration.** The variation of refractive index with wavelength (dispersion, §2.6) causes rays of different wavelengths to travel slightly different paths through an optical system, producing images at different positions along the axis (**longitudinal** or **axial chromatic aberration**) and/or at different lateral sizes (**lateral** or **transverse chromatic aberration**). These are also called **axial color** and **lateral color** respectively.

<!-- === PAGE 26 === -->

**6.10.2 Surface contributions.** The first-order chromatic aberration contribution of any surface may be found by differentiating Equation 5-(57) with respect to index, assuming the j−1th surfaces are unaberrated ($du_{-1} = 0$):

$$i' = yc + u \tag{33a}$$

(paraxial angle of incidence, limiting value of $i'$ from Eq. 5-35 for small angles).

The change in $u$ at the $j$th surface due to a change in index $dn$:

$$du = i \frac{n_{-1}}{n} \left[\frac{dn_{-1}}{n_{-1}} - \frac{dn}{n}\right]$$

**6.10.3 Total chromatic aberration.** The image displacement at the final plane due to a chromatic change at surface $j$:

$$dy_k = yn_{-1} i \left[\Delta\frac{dn}{n}\right] \Big/ (n_{k-1} u_{k-1}) = -\frac{a}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \tag{34}$$

$$d\bar{y}_k = yn_{-1} \bar{i} \left[\Delta\frac{dn}{n}\right] \Big/ (n_{k-1} u_{k-1}) = -\frac{b}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \tag{35}$$

$$d\bar{y}_k = dy_k \cdot \frac{\bar{i}}{i} \tag{36}$$

where $\Delta(dn/n)$ is defined as $(dn/n) - (dn_{-1}/n_{-1})$, the difference in $dn/n$ across the surface.

Since all surface contributions are differentials, they are directly additive. The total chromatic aberrations are:

$$\text{TAch} = \frac{-1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} a_j \tag{37}$$

$$\text{Tch} = \frac{-1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} b_j \tag{38}$$

where TAch = **transverse axial chromatic aberration** and Tch = **transverse chromatic aberration** (lateral color).

<!-- === PAGE 27 === -->

**6.10.4 Particular wavelengths used to calculate chromatic aberration.** Two wavelengths $\lambda_v$ (violet) and $\lambda_r$ (red) are chosen at the ends of the visible region. A reference wavelength $\lambda_g$ is chosen in the middle. Then:

$$\text{TAch}_{v-r} = (y_k)_v - (y_k)_r, \quad \text{Tch}_{v-r} = (\bar{y}_k)_v - (\bar{y}_k)_r$$

For **visual optical systems**, the standard wavelengths are:

$$n_v = n_F \quad (486.1\text{ nm}), \quad n_g = n_D \quad (589.3\text{ nm}), \quad n_r = n_C \quad (656.3\text{ nm})$$

**6.10.5 Graphical interpretation of axial and lateral color.**

**6.10.5.1** For a simple (undercorrected) positive lens, F-light focuses closer to the lens than D-light. The transverse axial chromatic blur $dy_{F-D}$ is a linear function of $y_1$ (height of the axial ray at entrance pupil):

$$\text{TAch}_{F-D} = (LA_{F-D}) u_{k-1} = -(LA_{F-D}) \frac{y_1}{f'} \tag{from Fig.~6.14}$$

where $LA_{F-D}$ = longitudinal axial chromatic aberration (separation of F and D focal planes).

$$\text{Tch}_{F-D}^* = \text{Tch}_{F-D} + Q \cdot \text{TAch} \tag{39}$$

where $Q = \bar{y}_1^*/y_1$.

- Equal $\bar{y}_k$ → lateral color corrected (same image height for both wavelengths)

**6.10.7 Chromatic aberration in a thin lens.** For a thin lens of power $\phi$ and Abbe number $\nu_{v-r}$ in a system at heights $y$ and $\bar{y}$:

$$\text{TAch}_{v-r} = \frac{1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \left(y^2 \frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right) \tag{from~Eq.~40}$$

$$\text{Tch}_{v-r} = \frac{1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \left(y \bar{y} \frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right) \tag{from~Eq.~41}$$

For a **system of $\eta$ thin lenses in air**:

$$\text{TAch}_{v-r} = \frac{1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \sum_{j=1}^{\eta} \left(y^2 \frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right)_j = \frac{-\Sigma a}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \tag{40}$$

$$\text{Tch}_{v-r} = \frac{1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \sum_{j=1}^{\eta} \left(y\bar{y} \frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right)_j = \frac{-\Sigma b}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \tag{41}$$

**6.10.8 Thin lens achromatic system.**

**6.10.8.1** For a doublet of two closely spaced thin lenses (a) and (b) in air:

$$\text{TAch}_{v-r} = \frac{1}{n_{k-1} u_{k-1}} \left[y^2 \left(\frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right)_a + y^2 \left(\frac{\phi}{\nu_{v-r}}\right)_b\right] \tag{42}$$

Setting $\text{TAch}_{v-r} = 0$:

$$\left(\frac{\phi}{\nu}\right)_a = -\left(\frac{\phi}{\nu}\right)_b \tag{43}$$

With $\phi = \phi_a + \phi_b$ for two thin lenses in contact, Eq. (43) gives the **achromatic doublet power equations**:

$$\phi_a = \phi \frac{\nu_a}{\nu_a - \nu_b} \tag{44}$$

$$\phi_b = -\phi \frac{\nu_b}{\nu_a - \nu_b} \tag{45}$$

<!-- === PAGE 33 === -->

The **Abbe number** (V-number):

$$\nu_{v-r} = \frac{n_g - 1}{n_v - n_r} \tag{46}$$

For visual systems ($n_v = n_F$, $n_g = n_D$, $n_r = n_C$): $\nu = (n_D - 1)/(n_F - n_C)$.

**6.10.8.3** Equations (44) and (45) enable one to pick two glasses with different $\nu$-values and calculate the powers to make an achromatic lens. The individual element powers can be kept small by selecting glasses with large $\Delta\nu = |\nu_a - \nu_b|$. Doublets should have $\Delta\nu > 20$.

**Secondary spectrum.** The residual chromatic aberration between $\lambda_v$ and intermediate wavelength $\lambda_g$ (after F-C achromatization) is:

$$\text{TAch}_{v-g} = -\frac{y}{n_{k-1}} \cdot \frac{\tilde{P}_a - \tilde{P}_b}{\nu_a - \nu_b} \tag{49}$$

where the **partial dispersion ratio** is:

$$\tilde{P} = \frac{n_v - n_g}{n_v - n_r}, \quad \nu_{v-g} = \nu_{v-r} / \tilde{P} \tag{48}$$

For almost any combination of ordinary glasses,

$$\frac{\tilde{P}_a - \tilde{P}_b}{\nu_a - \nu_b} \approx -\frac{1}{2200},$$

so by Equation (49) $\text{TAch}_{v-g}$ is positive for positive $y$, and D light focuses closer to the lens than F and C by about $f'/2200$ (scan p.6-36).

This is the **secondary spectrum** — it scales linearly with focal length regardless of glass choice, making it a fundamental limit for visual telescope objectives.

## 6.11 Entrance and Exit Pupils, the Chief Ray, and Vignetting

**6.11.2 The aperture stop.** The **aperture stop** is the physical stop or diaphragm that limits the bundle of rays passing through the system from any given object point. It may be a lens rim, a physical diaphragm, or any other physical boundary. It determines the solid angle of the ray bundle accepted from each object point.

<!-- === PAGE 37 === -->

**6.11.3 Entrance and exit pupils.**

**6.11.3.1** The **entrance pupil** is the image of the aperture stop in the part of the system preceding it; it is the apparent aperture as seen from the object side. To locate it, an axial paraxial ray is traced backwards through the system from the center of the aperture stop. The entrance pupil plane is where this ray last crosses the axis.

**6.11.3.2** The **exit pupil** is the image of the aperture stop in the part of the system following it. It is located by tracing an axial ray forward from the stop through the remaining elements.

**6.11.4 The chief ray.** The **chief ray** is an oblique ray from an off-axis object point that passes through the center of the aperture stop (and hence through the center of both pupils). It is the central ray of the oblique bundle and is representative of the entire bundle from that field point.

**6.11.5 Two convenient paraxial rays.** The standard procedure:
- Axial ray: $y_0 = 0$, $u_0 = $ half the cone angle from the object to the entrance pupil.
- Chief ray: $\bar{y}_0 \neq 0$, $\bar{u}_0 = \bar{y}_0 / t_{\text{object-to-pupil}}$, chosen so that $\bar{y} = 0$ at the aperture stop.

**6.11.6 Pupils as surfaces in the optical system.** Many designers include the entrance pupil plane as surface 1 in the ray trace. The exit pupil is surface $k-1$. Their inclusion is helpful because aberrations are conveniently described in terms of entrance pupil coordinates (pupil-plane heights). For distortion correction: $y_a \phi_a + y_b \phi_b = 0$ (ensures the chief ray changes angle equally and oppositely, maintaining image straightness).

### Gem 6.2 — Two-Ray Rule: Any Third Ray is a Linear Combination (§6.4)

**Statement:** After tracing any two linearly independent paraxial rays, any third paraxial ray satisfies:
$$\tilde{y} = A\bar{y} + By, \quad \tilde{u} = A\bar{u} + Bu$$

**In practice:** Trace the axial (marginal) ray $y$, $nu$ and the chief ray $\bar{y}$, $n\bar{u}$. All other rays — at any pupil zone and field angle — follow as linear combinations.

**Consequence:** Two paraxial ray traces give ALL first-order system properties: $f'$, magnification, pupil positions, principal planes, chromatic coefficients.

**Status:** Verified against scan §§6.4.1–6.4.3. Content accurate.

### Gem 6.3 — Graphical Ray Tracing Accuracy Limit (§6.1.2)

**Rule:** Graphical (drawing-board) ray tracing is limited to approximately **1% accuracy**.

**Implication:** Use graphical tracing for layout and visualization only. For any quantitative design result — aberration calculations, tolerance analysis, pupil sizing — use numerical paraxial ray tracing.

**Modern equivalent:** Free-hand sketching or rough CAD without proper surface modeling; the 1% bound applies similarly.
